2Each step in turn

The same ten steps, with what goes in and what comes out.

1
Market data arrives

Upstox publishes one completed candle per stock per day — the opening price, the highest, the lowest, the closing price, and how many shares traded. Five numbers, 50 stocks. That is the entire raw input to this system.

in —
out 50 candles
when after 15:30
2
We collect it

A single small program wakes at 16:00, makes one request, and writes down what it got. It records the time it asked and a version number, so if anyone questions a forecast months later we can reproduce the exact data it was built on.

in 50 candles
out 50 stamped rows
on Lambda
3
We check it

Fourteen rules run before anything is trusted: missing days, zero volume, impossible prices, and corporate actions. If a company splits its shares one into five, the price drops 80% overnight without anyone losing a rupee — left unadjusted, the model would learn a crash that never happened. If a check fails, the pipeline stops. No forecast is better than a wrong one.

in 50 raw rows
out 50 clean rows
or halt + alert
4
We store it

Clean data goes into S3 and stays there. Six years of daily history for all 50 stocks, which is a surprisingly small amount of data — a few hundred megabytes. Everything downstream reads from here, and nothing else is treated as the truth.

in clean rows
out 6 yr history
on S3
5
We build features

Five raw numbers tell a model almost nothing. So we calculate 40 richer ones from them — how much it moved this week, how choppy it has been, whether it is above or below its own average, whether volume is unusual, and what the wider market did. This is the step that does the real work, and it is where most of the engineering effort goes.

in 5 numbers
out 40 features
on Glue
6
We train four models

Once a week we show the computer every past day we have — about 50,000 rows — each one pairing what we knew that morning with what actually happened five days later. It finds the patterns itself. Four models get trained, because we are asking four different questions. Takes five minutes.

in 50,000 rows
out 4 model files
when Sunday 02:00
7
We run the forecast

Every morning at 08:30 the four trained models are loaded and today’s 50 rows are fed through them. Out come 200 numbers — for each stock: where it should close, the highest it should reach, the lowest it should dip, and the odds it ends higher. Three minutes, then the machine switches off.

in 50 × 40
out 200 numbers
when daily 08:30
8
We fix the odds

The fourth model returns a score between 0 and 1, and it is tempting to call that a probability — but it is not one yet. Left alone it is systematically overconfident. This step corrects it against history, so that when the screen says 70%, those days really did go up about 70% of the time.

in raw score
out true probability
gate error ≤ 0.10
9
The safety gate

Last check before anything is shown. Do the three price predictions agree with each other — is the low actually below the close, and the close below the high? Is the expected move big enough to beat trading costs of about 0.3%? Anything that fails is labelled NO TRADE and displayed as such, never quietly hidden.

in 200 numbers
out approved
or NO TRADE
10
A person reads it

The 50 forecasts appear on a private dashboard behind a login. Each one shows its target, its stop level, and its probability — and next to every number, how accurate the model has actually been lately compared with simply guessing “up” every day. The line ends here.

in approved
out a decision
by a human

3What step 7 actually produces

Four questions, four answers, for every one of the 50 stocks.

Why four models and not one

They are genuinely different questions. The highest point over five days is a maximum, not an average — and a model trained to predict averages will guess it too low every single time. The same applies to the lowest point, in the other direction. And “will it go up, yes or no” is a different kind of question altogether.

Together they give you a target, a stop level and a confidence, which is what you actually need to decide anything. A single closing-price guess tells you where, but nothing about how far it might swing on the way.

Total compute on a normal weekday is about nine minutes. Everything else sits idle, which is why the monthly bill lands near $121 rather than the ~$375 an always-on design would cost.

One thing that often confuses people

The live price feed during market hours is not part of the ten steps. It drives the moving numbers on the screen and is then discarded. The forecast is calculated once a day, before the market opens, from yesterday’s completed data.

Live prices feed the eyes. Completed daily data feeds the model.

What this system does not do

It does not place orders, connect to a broker, hold money, or give investment advice. The line ends at step 10 with a person reading a screen. Every forecast is shown alongside how accurate the model has actually been, so a modest edge is never dressed up as a large one.
