A

Where the stock data comes from

Three sources, one of which does all the real work.

Everything downstream depends on getting clean price history. That history comes from Upstox, an Indian broker whose API gives free read-only market data on an Analytics Token — valid for one year, generated once, and with no static IP requirement for market data, history or the live feed.

SourceWhat it givesHow oftenCost
Upstox APIDaily candle per stock: open, high, low, close, volumeOnce daily, after closeFree
Upstox WebSocketLive prices during market hours — display onlyContinuous 09:15–15:30Free
NSE bhavcopyDelivery percentage, corporate actionsOnce dailyFree
NIFTY 50 indexMarket context (via Upstox)Once dailyFree
The distinction that confuses everyone

There are two data paths from Upstox and they do completely different jobs.

The live feed streams prices every second while the market is open. It drives the moving numbers on the dashboard and is then discarded. Nothing is stored and no prediction uses it.

The daily candle is one settled row per stock, fetched once after the market closes. This is the only data the model ever sees.

Put simply: live prices feed the eyes, completed daily data feeds the brain.

1
The market closes and data becomes final

At 15:30 IST trading stops. Shortly after, Upstox publishes the completed candle for each stock — the price it opened at, the highest and lowest it traded, the price it closed at, and how many shares changed hands.

Five numbers per stock. With 50 stocks, that is 250 numbers for the entire trading day.

out 50 candles
when after 15:30
2
A small program collects them

At 16:00 an AWS Lambda wakes up, makes one API request, and writes down what came back. It records the time it asked and a version number alongside the data.

That bookkeeping matters: if someone questions a forecast three months later, we can prove exactly which data it was built from. A forecast you cannot reproduce is a forecast you cannot defend.

3
Fourteen checks before anything is trusted

Missing days, zero volume, impossible prices, and above all corporate actions. If a company splits its shares one into five, the price falls 80% overnight while nobody loses a rupee. Left unadjusted, the model would learn a crash that never happened.

If a check fails, the pipeline stops. No forecast is better than a confident wrong one.

out 50 clean
or halt + alert
B

Where we store it, and how it is prepared

Two very different storage jobs, which is why there are two different services.

People expect one database. This system has two stores because it has two unrelated problems: keeping a lot of history cheaply, and answering a web page quickly.

Amazon S3Aurora PostgreSQL
Holds5 years of daily candles, all featuresToday's 50 forecasts, accuracy history
SizeTens of gigabytesA few megabytes
Read byTraining and feature jobs, in bulkThe dashboard, one query at a time
Good atStoring enormous amounts for almost nothingAnswering a specific question in milliseconds
Bad atAnswering "show me RELIANCE today" quicklyHolding bulk history — costs many times more
Cost~$3/month~$45/month

Using one for the other's job is the classic mistake: forecasts in S3 means scanning files to render a page, and training history in Aurora means paying database prices for cold storage.

Clean data lands in S3 and stays there

Stored as Parquet, partitioned by date. Five years of history for 50 stocks is a surprisingly small amount of data — a few hundred megabytes. This is the single source of truth; nothing else is treated as authoritative.

5
Five raw numbers become forty features

This is the step that does the real work. "Close = 2441" tells a model nothing — it is just a price. So an AWS Glue job calculates 40 measurements from the raw candles: how much it moved this week, how choppy it has been, whether it sits above or below its own averages, whether volume is unusual, and what the wider market did.

Everything is a ratio or a percentage, never a rupee price. That way a row from 2021 is directly comparable with one from 2026, and a ₹200 stock with a ₹3,000 one.

A detail that costs you a year of history

Some features need a long run-up: a 200-day average needs 200 prior days, and 52-week range needs a full year. So the first ~250 rows of every stock cannot be used — there is nothing to calculate from.

Fetch 6 years, lose the first one to warm-up, and you are left with roughly 50,000 usable rows (50 stocks × ~1,000 days). Plan for that number, not the raw count.

C

How the prediction is calculated

The part everyone asks about. It is more ordinary than it sounds.

What "training" actually means

There is no stock-prediction service to call. No API returns tomorrow's price, and a general AI like ChatGPT or Claude cannot do this — it has no access to market data and was never built for it. The model has to be trained on our own data.

XGBoost arrives as an empty algorithm. It knows how to find patterns in tables; it knows nothing about stocks, RSI or RELIANCE. All of its knowledge comes from the 50,000 rows we show it.

One training row — what the model is shown

-------------------------------        ------------------------------
ret_1d          +0.44%                 closed at    +0.72%
ret_5d          +1.82%                 highest hit  +2.65%
rsi_14           58.3                  lowest dip   -0.10%
vol_ratio        1.18                  ended up?    yes
dist_ema50      +2.10%
nifty_ret_1d    +0.31%
delivery_pct     48.2%

                 ^                                   ^
         the 40 inputs                     the 4 answers to learn

Show the computer 50,000 of these pairs and it works out which input patterns tend to precede which outcomes. That is all training is. It is not reasoning about companies or reading the news — it is pattern-matching across history.

How XGBoost finds those patterns

It builds hundreds of small decision trees, where each new tree corrects the mistakes of the ones before it.

Boosting — why one weak tree becomes a strong model
True answer: +2.00%

  Tree 1 guesses +0.50%   -> still 1.50% short
  Tree 2 studies only that 1.50% leftover, guesses +0.90%   -> 0.60% short
  Tree 3 studies only that 0.60% leftover, guesses +0.40%   -> 0.20% short
  Tree 4 studies only that 0.20% leftover, guesses +0.10%   -> 0.10% short
  ...
  Tree 300

  Final = 0.50 + 0.90 + 0.40 + 0.10 + ... = +1.95%

One tree alone is poor. Three hundred, each fixing the last one's error, is strong. We use XGBoost rather than a neural network because it is consistently better on table-shaped data, trains in minutes on an ordinary CPU, and can report which features mattered — which matters when a person has to explain a forecast.

Why four models and not one

Same 40 inputs each time. Only the answer being learned differs.

ModelTypeLearnsWhy it needs its own model
1. CloseRegressor5-day return of the closing priceThe baseline question — where it lands
2. HighQuantile 0.90Highest point over 5 daysA maximum is not an average. A normal model guesses it too low every time.
3. LowQuantile 0.10Lowest point over 5 daysSame problem, inverted. Your stop-loss depends on this.
4. ProbabilityClassifierWill it end higher — yes or noA different kind of question entirely, needing calibration

Together they give a target, a stop level and a confidence — which is what you need to decide anything. A single closing-price guess tells you where it might land but nothing about how far it could swing on the way.

6
Training, once a week

Every Sunday at 02:00 a SageMaker Training Job fits all four models on the full history. About five minutes and roughly $0.50 a run. The output is four files of about 2 MB each — just lists of learned if-statements — saved to S3 with a version number.

Training uses walk-forward splits with a mandatory 5-day gap: always train on the past and test on the future, never the reverse, and leave a gap so a model cannot glimpse its own answer.

in 50,000 rows
out 4 model files
when Sun 02:00
7
Forecasting, once a day

At 08:30 a SageMaker Batch Transform job loads the four saved files and feeds in today's 50 feature rows. Out come 200 numbers — four per stock. Three minutes, then the machine shuts down.

Nothing is learned here. The models are read-only, which is why a day's forecast can always be traced back to the exact file that produced it.

in 50 × 40
out 200 numbers
when daily 08:30
8
Turning a score into a real probability

Model 4 returns a number between 0 and 1, and it is tempting to call that a probability. It is not one yet — left alone it is systematically overconfident.

So we check it against history: take every past day the model said "0.70" and count how many actually rose. If it was 57%, the mapping is corrected until 70% on the screen genuinely means 70%. The gate is an Expected Calibration Error at or below 0.10 — above that, the forecast is not published.

in raw score
out true probability
gate ECE ≤ 0.10
The mistake that produces fake 99% accuracy

If you train a model to predict the price, it scores beautifully — R² above 0.99 — and is completely useless. It has learned "RELIANCE is around ₹2,400", which is true and tells you nothing about tomorrow.

Train on the change, never the level. Every one of the 40 features and all four answers in this system are ratios or percentages for exactly this reason.

D

How it reaches the screen

One gate, one small database, one page behind a login.

9
The safety gate

Last check before anything is shown, running as plain Python in a Lambda:

Do the numbers agree? The low must be below the close, and the close below the high. The three price models are independent, so they can disagree — and catching that is a free bug detector.

Is it worth doing? Brokerage, STT and slippage cost about 0.30% per round trip. A predicted move of 0.20% is a loss even if the direction is right.

Anything failing either test is labelled NO TRADE and shown as such — never quietly hidden.

in 200 numbers
out approved
or NO TRADE
10
A person reads it

Approved forecasts are written to Aurora. A static site on CloudFront calls API Gateway, which reads that table. Cognito with MFA guards the login.

Each forecast shows its target, its stop level and its probability — and permanently beside them, how accurate the model has actually been lately compared with simply guessing "up" every day.

The line ends here. There is no step 11.

5What to expect, honestly

The section to read before showing this to anyone.

MeasureRealisticRed flagWhat the red flag means
Directional accuracy53–56%above 60%Almost certainly a bug, not a breakthrough
Edge over baseline+1 to +3 pointsabove +5Check the train/test split for leakage
R² on returnsnear zeroabove 0.10You are probably predicting price, not change
Calibration error≤ 0.10> 0.15The probabilities are not trustworthy yet
Say this out loud to any audience

Realistic accuracy for short-horizon direction on liquid large-cap equities is roughly 52–56%. Published claims of 95%+ almost always come from measuring the wrong thing — usually predicting the price level rather than the change, which scores beautifully while predicting nothing useful.

If this system ever reports above 60% on unseen data, the correct first response is to look for a bug. In this field, results that are too good are far more often mistakes than discoveries.

A legitimate outcome

It is entirely possible that the models show no reliable edge. Markets are close to efficient and that result is expected often enough to plan for.

Measuring honestly and reporting "no edge found" is a successful outcome for this project — considerably better than shipping a model that only looked good because the test was wrong. The AWS architecture, the pipeline and the discipline are what the demo is actually showing.

What this system does not do

It does not place orders, connect to a broker for trading, hold money, or give investment advice. There is no execution path. The system ends at step 10 with a person reading a screen and deciding for themselves.
